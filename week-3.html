Massachusetts and Boston ADU Rules and a Proposed ADU Screening Tool

Massachusetts and Boston have both made accessory dwelling units (ADUs) easier to build, through different laws, but neither set of rules tells a homeowner what they can build on a particular lot.

Week 3 · September 28, 2026

I argue that our class should build a tool that tells owners of existing one- to three-unit homes which ADU types their lot likely allows by right and which building-code requirements would add cost.

1. Comparing the Massachusetts and Boston ADU Rules

The most important difference is that the state ADU law does not apply to Boston. The Affordable Homes Act amended Chapter 40A, the state Zoning Act, to require every other city and town to allow one “Protected Use ADU” by right on qualifying lots. The state’s housing office (EOHLC) notes that Boston zones under its own enabling act (St. 1956, c. 665) and sets its own ADU rules. Table 1 compares the two.

Location matters more in Boston. The state law sets one minimum standard for 350 municipalities. Boston’s rules vary by zoning article and overlay district, and draft “Dormer” and “Decker” districts for Roslindale, West Roxbury, and Hyde Park, which would allow all three ADU types, are open for comment this fall. A Boston owner outside Mattapan who follows state guidance would wrongly conclude that a detached ADU is allowed by right. Our Mattapan exercises showed that even one neighborhood requires reading Article 60’s ADU provisions with the Article 2 definitions and Table D.

The two frameworks were also written for different housing. The state law covers single-family zoning districts and sizes the ADU relative to the main house. The Metropolitan Area Planning Council (MAPC) reports that more than half of the 1,224 ADUs permitted statewide in 2025 were detached. Much of Boston’s housing is two- and three-family houses on small lots, so Boston’s rules also cap total units per lot. In Mattapan’s first two years under new zoning, most by-right projects were inside existing buildings, with 17 basement conversions compared with 9 additions and 1 new building.

The frameworks also treat existing buildings differently. State regulations bar towns from blocking a Protected Use ADU because a house or lot is nonconforming. In Boston, most buildings already exceed the base zoning limits, so whether an ADU is allowed depends on how much lot coverage, floor area, and height the current building already uses. Boston’s newer rules set separate limits for existing homes. Mattapan allows lots with ADUs to cover 50–60% of the lot, and the draft Decker-4 district lets existing homes that add units reach 45 feet and four stories. The state building code also affects Boston more, because an internal ADU in a two-family house creates a third unit and moves the building from the IRC to the IBC. An owner therefore needs to know their zoning district and overlays, their owner-occupancy status, the existing building’s size and unit count, and the code requirements for their ADU type.

2. What Makes These Rules Difficult to Navigate

The first challenge is that Boston’s base zoning does not match the buildings that already exist, and most ADUs are added to existing buildings. Will Cohen’s September 21 presentation showed me how large this mismatch is. His team found that 99% of Boston’s residential lots have buildings larger than current zoning allows. Most of these buildings predate the 1964 zoning code. He also showed a finished basement that needed ZBA approval because it exceeded the floor-area ratio limit, although the exterior did not change. Gray argues that as-of-right approval gives “a high degree of certainty about what can and can’t be built,” while discretionary review is “onerous and unpredictable” and adds fees, attorney costs, hearings, and delay. Cohen’s draft zoning rewrites the limits to match existing houses (“zoning that looks like our houses”), but owners will have to measure their buildings against them.

The second challenge is that building and fire codes, which can add major costs, do not appear on a zoning map. Sarah Dunbar’s firm, Neighbor Architects, designed all twelve prototypes in the ADU Guidebook and labeled each with its complexity, building code, stairs, and sprinkler requirements. Her designs showed me how much these requirements vary by ADU type. Adding a floor to a two-unit house moves the whole building to the IBC and requires sprinklers throughout. A backyard cottage stays under the residential code, but because it sits behind the house, more than 25 feet from the street, it needs sprinklers and a 10-foot path clear of cars and trees. A basement unit needs 7′6″ of headroom and bedroom egress windows and is not allowed in the Coastal Flood Resiliency Overlay.

The third challenge begins after zoning allows an ADU. MAPC’s Living Little notes that ADUs are most often built by “regular homeowners, rather than professional developers,” which makes them hard to finance. MAPC’s presentation put the minimum cost at about $200,000, and Boston’s 0% ADU loan covers at most $50,000 of construction. Mattapan rezoned about 3,600 parcels, but only ten projects added units by right in the first two years, and Boston permitted 34 ADUs citywide in 2024. Cohen and his colleague Kathleen Onufer say the bigger early effect was faster permitting for renovations and for legalizing units people already lived in. Manville, Lens, and Monkkonen call upzoning “necessary but not sufficient.” They note that in Freemark’s Chicago study, upzoned parcels gained value but were not redeveloped, and they attribute this mainly to condominium ownership. For ADUs, the comparable obstacle is homeowners’ limited money and technical knowledge. A Jamaica Plain resident quoted in the Guidebook asked for “clear guidelines… on a predictable budget and timeline.”

3. What a Tool Could Help With

The primary user is an owner-occupant of an existing one- to three-unit Boston home. Their question is, “Given my house and lot, which ADUs can I build without a ZBA variance, how big can they be, and what will make them complicated or expensive?” Secondary users include City ADU staff and community groups.

Existing tools mostly provide information. Boston’s zoning viewer and the Neighborhood Housing Look Up report a parcel’s zoning district, and the Guidebook offers designs but “does not provide pre-approved plans.” The state’s MyMassADU website lets owners explore options by address, but for most of Boston it says that attached and detached ADUs face “a much more in-depth zoning and permitting review process.” An independent open-source tool for West Roxbury compares current and proposed rules for each parcel but does not evaluate ADU options.

The tool would combine parcel, building, and zoning data with a few facts the owner confirms, such as unit count, owner occupancy, and basement height. It would test five ADU types from the Guidebook (basement, attic or dormer, attached addition, detached backyard unit, and garage conversion) and report for each whether it is likely allowed by right, likely needs ZBA relief and why, or is not possible. It would show the lot coverage and floor area still available, flag code and cost issues (“this would be a third unit, so expect IBC review and sprinklers”), and produce a one-page summary of data, assumptions, and questions for an architect or the Inspectional Services Department (ISD). Where current tools report “your district is Mattapan R2,” this tool might report that a basement unit is the most likely by-right option and that the side yard is too narrow for the 10-foot path a detached unit needs.

The tool should not issue zoning determinations, which are ISD’s responsibility, and it should not certify code compliance, predict ZBA votes, produce designs, give financial advice, or screen tenants. Setbacks estimated from building footprints are less precise than a survey, so the tool should flag results close to a limit and recommend a survey.

4. What We Would Need to Build It

The first task is structuring the zoning rules. Article 60’s ADU provisions and Table D, the citywide internal-ADU rules, and the draft Neighborhood Housing tables must become machine-readable rules that cite code sections and note whether each is draft or adopted. The Guidebook’s sprinkler and fire-access checklists can become a decision tree, and the City’s ADU loan program could supply cost and eligibility data. For MAPC, the same database could hold the state rules and local bylaws from its Zoning Atlas.

Most parcel data already exists. Public datasets cover parcels, zoning and overlay districts, building footprints, and assessor records (units, year built, building type). We would calculate lot coverage, setbacks, side-yard widths, and each building’s distance from the street, as Cohen’s team and the West Roxbury tool have done.

We would also need a database linking each parcel to its building, zoning district, and rule version; a rules engine that explains each result; API connections to City GIS services; and a simple multilingual interface. We should run the tool on past cases, such as Mattapan’s by-right additions and recent ZBA decisions, have Planning Department staff and an architect or code consultant review the encoded rules, and test with Mattapan homeowners whether they understand a “likely by-right” result.

Several uncertainties remain. Draft districts may change after this fall’s comment period, assessor records contain errors, and some rules need interpretation, such as what counts as a half-story. Code requirements depend on design, cost data is scarce, and, as MAPC noted, ADU tracking is inconsistent.

Recommendation

The class should focus on helping owner-occupants of existing one- to three-unit homes learn which ADU types are likely allowed by right on their lot, how much of the zoning limits their building already uses, and which code requirements a design would trigger. I would start with a pilot in Mattapan. It is the only Boston neighborhood where all three ADU types are allowed by right, it has two years of results to test against, and statewide tools give Boston owners only general guidance. Storing the zoning rules separately from the parcel data would let the same tool add the Neighborhood Housing districts once they are adopted, or use the state rules if MAPC is our client. I would judge the tool by whether more owners bring feasible ADU projects to an architect.
