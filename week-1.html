Same Block, Three Different Problems

Take one case. A neighborhood where households cannot get reliable internet at home. Relabel the work three times and watch the problem itself change underneath you.

Week 1 · September 15, 2026

Take a neighborhood where a lot of households cannot get reliable internet at home. Everyone agrees that is a problem. Almost nobody agrees on what the problem is. Ask a civic technologist, then somebody who works in public interest technology, then a community technologist, and you get three different projects for the same block, each with its own budget and its own definition of winning.

That is the argument. These three labels are not synonyms and they are not describing the software, because the software barely matters here. The label picks the problem. Everything downstream, the build, the money, the scorecard, follows from that first choice, and the first choice is made by whoever holds the label.

McGuinness and Schank make a version of this point with open data, borrowing it from Harlan Yu and David Robinson. Budapest and Szeged publish clean machine-readable transit schedules while Hungary slides toward authoritarianism. Open, governmental, and no help whatsoever. You cannot read the politics off the artifact.

Civic technology names a missing map

Under this framing, outside technologists own the problem statement, and the problem is that nobody has good data on where service actually fails. So a group builds a speed-test app, publishes a coverage map, and hands it to reporters and the city council. McGuinness and Schank describe the posture exactly, technologists working “outside of or adjacent to government” and using open data “to pressure government to be more responsive,” and they refuse the label for their own work because it keeps an “outsider hacker patina.” Success arrives fast and photographs well. The map is live. The data is public. The story ran. Nobody here has standing to say the project missed, because the people who defined the problem are the same people grading it, and they can move to the next city whenever they like.

Public interest technology makes it the city’s problem

This framing starts somewhere else entirely, because its founding claim is that government is the instrument. “There is no solving the world’s hardest problems without governments and institutions that really work for people,” McGuinness and Schank write, and their preface closes on the line that the role of government is to help all people. So the problem gets restated as something a city can own. Residents cannot navigate the subsidy enrollment, or the city has no digital inclusion program worth the name. The city stands up a broadband office, redesigns how people sign up, and measures adoption, which is exactly the design, data and delivery loop the book is built around. Real improvement. Also a quiet substitution, because the block is no longer the client. The city is, and the city decides when it has done enough.

Community technology asks who owns the wire

Hand the problem statement to the neighborhood and the answer changes shape. The problem is not the map and not the enrollment form. It is that the block depends on infrastructure nobody who lives there controls. #MoreThanCode lists building a community-controlled mesh network among its practitioner success stories and points to Detroit, Philadelphia and Red Hook in New York as models worth replicating. The report also argues that infrastructure projects like these are the best openings to “create citywide coalitions, connect diverse actors, and build community power.” What gets built is a network plus a set of neighbors trained to keep it running. Success is the kind that reads badly in a grant report. The network works, and the people on the block maintain it after the technologist goes home.

Practitioners already know this

None of that is a thought experiment I imposed on the readings. The #MoreThanCode researchers handed 96 practitioners a worksheet of terms, asked which ones they claimed, and got 252 terms back. Eighteen claimed “public interest technology.” Civic tech and community technology got 28 each, and terms about access and digital literacy were circled 131 times. The report concludes that these differences “are important and should not be erased.” The reasons people gave back that up. Stevie, a foundation fellow, dislikes “civic tech” and “technology for social good” because both “put the technology first, rather than the people.” Arata, who builds technical capacity at nonprofits, hears “public interest” as a Washington word that “connotes policy, power, and privilege.” One person made the comparison I keep coming back to. Law has public interest law and it also has movement lawyering. Overlapping, not the same job.

It matters who coined the word. McGuinness and Schank say outright that “public interest technology” was modeled on public interest law, and that the Ford Foundation set out to build the field the same way it had built the legal one decades earlier. The term arrived from a funder. Funders are exactly where the practitioners’ complaint lands. “Funders control the frame,” the report says, and people described rewriting grant applications in whatever language was getting money that year while the money itself concentrated in national organizations run by white men. So the three versions of this block are not equally fundable, and fundability has nothing to do with which one would help.

Where this is heading

McGuinness and Schank end their history by deciding the naming fight is “unnecessarily complex,” and land on “the most important part, we believe, is the doing.” Then look at what they describe a few pages earlier. The Public Interest Technology University Network grew from twenty-one universities to thirty-six in a single year, and that network decides which courses run and which fellowships have money behind them. The name is doing plenty of work for something they say hardly matters.

The bottom row of the chart is the one I would keep. When the thing goes wrong, who is allowed to say so? Under the civic tech framing, nobody, because the people who set the problem can always go home. Public interest technology answers with the institution, which beats nothing and still leaves the city checking its own homework. Only #MoreThanCode puts somebody outside the building in that seat, recommending community design boards modeled on the review boards that sign off on research, under the slogan “nothing about us without us.” Procedural and unglamorous. Also the only one of the three that stops you from publishing a beautiful map of a neighborhood that still has no internet and calling it a win.
